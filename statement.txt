For the moment, as an initial basis for identification, let me just indicate that my family name begins with the letter “k” and that I grew up in France.

Context of my situation:

I used to be a top-performing student with a strong interest in science and in improving my cognition, and until 2019, when I was 15, I lived with the aim of maximizing it, pursuing expanded consciousness and deep concentration. Although my mental health wasn't great, I was still able to experience emotions, sometimes strong, and find enjoyment.

At that age, however, my longstanding tendency toward insomnia and my worsening mood led my parents and a psychiatrist of a local hospital unit to compel me to take medication.

I was prescribed a neuroleptic, cyamemazine, and an SSRI, sertraline.  
Although I reacted badly to them, experiencing a major reduction in wakefulness and ability to concentrate, and repeatedly pleaded to stop the treatment, I was forcibly kept (pressured and scrutinized at each intake) on the neuroleptic for one month and on the SSRI for eight months, during which the following events took place.

In what evidently appeared to be a result of my poor reaction to the SSRI, I suffered at least three head injuries in just 2 months, including one that caused a brief loss of consciousness, around four months after beginning the medication. The psychiatrist was away on holidays during that time, none of the injuries occurred in front of my parents, and on each occurrence the people around failed to acknowledge what had happened as a medical emergency that required taking me to the hospital - although it was considered after the second incident, during which I got knocked out and some of my teeth broke.  
As a result, I never received care for those head injuries, and none of them were officially documented - although I reported them to doctors in the following years.  
During the days following the most serious injury, I was also kept in a harmful and overstimulating environment: a sports camp involving three to four-hour intensive training sessions every day, with activities such as running and playing contact sport matches. This, from my understanding, is likely to have interfered with a recovery that would already have been affected by the SSRI.

That sequence of events left me feeling extremely fatigued, impulsive, depressed, and cognitively diminished, with a collection of symptoms that could be described as a frontal lobe syndrome. Numerous traces support this characterization, but they are both too personal and too extensive to expose and discuss here.

A few months after this sequence, acknowledging how much my condition had deteriorated, my mother finally allowed me to discontinue the SSRI.  
Stopping it, however, did not restore my cognitive abilities or my former level of consciousness as I had expected, having originally not thought much about the head injuries and attributed to the treatment the decline over the treatment period, since it had been dulling me from the beginning.  
Before this decline, I would experience strong, vivid emotions and senses, and enter flow states in which I experienced a broad and clear mental space, like a virtual environment where processed information was sharply defined, reliably recorded, and quickly accessible. After, my mind had instead become foggy, with any item appearing blurry, inconsistent, and difficult to use.  
This limited me in every kind of task, made mental manipulation difficult, degraded my experience of the world, and, among others, prevented me from reaching the wellbeing I had previously found through concentration.  
As already somewhat stated before, although my ordinary baseline state had not been that good before (and had degraded after the Summer of 2018), it didn't use to be too bad, and I would experience moments of happiness when I devoted myself to intellectual activities or met some fulfilling events in day-to-day life.  
Focus, expressed through projects and efforts to improve myself, had given my life meaning; I lost that meaning when I stopped being able to experience much pleasure through it, meaningfully pursue or accomplish any mental task, and started going through pain (fatigue and headaches) every time I tried.

From around the age of 12, my life had revolved around maximizing the cognitive aspects described above. In particular, I worked on expanding my field of perception and increasing my ability to process several pieces of information simultaneously, including while reading. I was committed to constructing a mental palace for myself and became so fixated on this pursuit that anecdotes in cultural material of cognitive diminution would terrify me, and I developed phobias concerning anything around me I could conceive as a risk.  
Thus, this extraordinary and apparently insurmountable setback, doubled with multiple side effects, leaving me crippled, destroyed the reason I had for living, and extinguished my desire to continue.

Today, life feels painful most of the time, very often intensely so. I continue to constantly suffer from what I regard as downstream consequences of what occurred in 2019, including excessive fatigability, a strong predisposition toward migraines, anhedonia, and other symptoms. It has been almost a decade since life last seemed worth living, yet I have fought to continue anyway after deciding in 2020, while severely depressed, that, provided the prospects of recovery with technological development, I should not already give up, and it would be rational to wait about 15 more years, in however poor conditions, to put all chances on my side.  
The likelihood that tech progress will restore me to a state of mental capacity within a sufficiently near future means that I could stop suffering relatively soon, start feeling alive and well again, and return to my goals of learning and self-improvement.  
I originally felt that I owed this to my former self, who was greater than who I am now. After going through so much pain, though, today, I feel like I'm mostly fighting to stick on because of the imminence of a breakthrough that could serve me, because of that planned expiry to my fight that puts a clear end date to my suffering - which, very notably, relieves the guilt of hurting the mental health of people that I matter for and abandoning them, by allowing me to think "I've gone to the furthest to not let them down" - and, at least most of the time, also because of the questions and fears about a means and how much worse it could make my state.  
All of that determination, however strong it used to be, is showing its limits when the pain grows too much and won't subside. 

Despite my initial decision to wait until 2035 to attain that recovery, if technological and medical progress stagnate, if my prospects of gaining access to a restorative treatment decline for a substantial length of time, or if my state forces me to extremities even stronger than before, I can't be certain how much longer I will be able to sustain survival.

As a bit of more concrete context on the past few years: partly with the intent to try to accumulate money that could support my recovery, and partly because of strong understimulation, I have gone back to being somewhat active again in real life in 2023. This activity, however, feels unsustainable and has intensified my pain - even though I would not describe myself as being in a panicked depressive state like I was in 2020; I'm now mostly apathetic and anhedonic.  
Attempting to move on and disregard this history has not helped, because I am plainly still weakened.

I have continued seeing the same psychiatrist since I changed psychiatrists at the end of 2019. That had happened shortly after stopping the antidepressant - which allowed me to come to an end with that experience with the conviction to rule that the previous psychiatrist had been highly detrimental - with the assistance of my mother, at that point. I've tried medications since - which I won't name for immediate privacy reasons - after a worsening of my state around January 2026.  
I have considered using pro-cognitive substances in the meantime, but at least at reasonable doses, they do not give me an ability to concentrate, learn, or feel that is qualitatively equivalent to what I had before (nor necessarily address the other symptoms). In any case, they do not represent a viable path forward.
